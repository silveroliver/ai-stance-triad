Parliament makes about 36 laws a year. Ministers make about 1,700.

An Act of Parliament is primary legislation: debated, amended and voted through both Houses over months. An Act almost always ends with a clause handing ministers the power to fill in the detail later. That detail is secondary legislation, and it usually arrives as a statutory instrument (SI). An SI is real, enforceable law. It can create criminal offences, set benefit rates and change what schools must do.

The two routes

Negative and affirmative: who has to say yes?

The enabling Act decides which route an instrument takes. That single choice, made years earlier when the Act was passed, decides whether Parliament has to actively approve the rule or merely fail to stop it.

Negative procedure

Used for roughly two thirds of SIs · Education and Families examples above
  1. A minister signs the instrument. It is law from that moment.
  2. It is laid before both Houses.
  3. A 40-day objection period begins.
  4. Committees may examine it and report.
  5. Any MP or peer may table a prayer to annul it — but the government controls whether that prayer is ever debated.
  6. If no prayer is agreed, the instrument simply remains law.
Silence is consent. Doing nothing is how most of these laws pass.

Affirmative procedure

Roughly a fifth to a quarter of SIs · Crime and Work examples above
  1. A draft is laid before both Houses. It is not yet law.
  2. The government tables a motion to approve it. Nothing can be debated until it does.
  3. Committees examine it and report.
  4. Each House debates and votes — often in committee rather than the chamber.
  5. Only once both Houses have approved can the minister sign it into law.
Consent must be given. But the vote is still yes or no on the whole text, and governments with a majority rarely lose.
Getting involved

Six things a person outside Parliament can actually do

Secondary legislation is unusually open to outside influence, precisely because so few people are watching it. The scrutiny committees in particular are hungry for evidence they cannot get from the department.

01

Answer the consultation

Most SIs are preceded by a government consultation on GOV.UK. This is the stage with the most leverage, because the text is still being written. Anyone can respond, including a sixth-form class.

02

Track the instrument

Parliament publishes a live page for every SI at statutoryinstruments.parliament.uk, and the full text sits on legislation.gov.uk. Every step in the walkthroughs above links to one of these.

03

Write to the scrutiny committees

The Lords Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee takes submissions from charities, professionals and the public, and quotes them in its reports. Drawing an instrument to the special attention of the House starts here.

04

Ask your MP to pray against it

Under the negative procedure any MP can table a prayer. It costs one signature. Getting it debated needs pressure — the Adoption walkthrough shows a prayer that did reach the floor.

05

Petition and organise

Petitions on petition.parliament.uk passing 100,000 signatures are considered for debate. Campaigning organisations are often the only reason an SI gets noticed at all.

06

Challenge it in court

Unlike an Act, an SI can be struck down by judges if the minister exceeded the power in the parent Act or failed to consult. This is what happened to the 2020 adoption regulations.
